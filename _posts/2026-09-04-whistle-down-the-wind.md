
_+ Q&A with actors Hayley Mills, Diane Poole and Alan Barnes_

Bryan Forbes’s first feature as director, _Whistle Down the Wind_ is undoubtedly a ‘classic’, if that word denotes a film that continues to enchant audiences over 60 years after its making, and which still resonates with those who saw it at an impressionable age. It evokes a certain time – both of life and in history – yet it bears few direct traces of the early months of 1961 when it was made. It’s also a film that is difficult to pigeonhole into a particular genre.

The film follows the three Bostock children, Kathy, Nan and Charles, who live on a farm in rural Lancashire with their father and aunt. One night, Kathy discovers a man in their barn and, hearing him utter the words ‘Jesus Christ’, concludes that he is the son of God. She convinces her siblings of his identity and they look after him, unaware that he is actually Arthur Alan Blakey, a suspected murderer on the run.

In telling this story, _Whistle Down the Wind_ makes exceptional use of non-professional child actors, whose naturalness and ‘rightness’, both in relation to character and setting, lend it a unique charm. Their naive logic and plain speaking imbue their interactions with an endearing humour, through which the film subtly exposes and challenges the hypocrisy of the adult world. In this way, its outward simplicity conceals complex ideas and themes that deserve exploration.

The screenplay of _Whistle Down the Wind_ bears the traces of the various hands that contributed the ingredients and moulded it into its final shape. The creative input came from two contrasting worlds: Mary Hayley Bell, wife of actor John Mills and centre of a cultural clique (of which producer Richard Attenborough and Forbes were also a part), and the more instinctively realist (and humanist) vision of Yorkshire-born writing partnership Keith Waterhouse and Willis Hall. These seemingly opposing worlds produced something that transcended the constraints and potential of both, creating a film with wide appeal to an audience spanning age and class.

Forbes later remarked, ‘given the eleventh-hour atmosphere, I have never ceased to be amazed at the ease with which the film went together’, crediting Attenborough’s hard work and efficiency, which is attested to by the correspondence in his personal archive housed at the University of Sussex. From scouting the area in early December 1960 to the start of filming on 13 February 1961, the team completed the selection and preparation of locations, cast both professional and non-professional actors, and organised accommodation, equipment, transport and the construction of a production village in a field near Worsaw End Farm, in the Pendle Valley, Lancashire.

The film’s final cost was £154,026, more than £10,000 under budget, and it was completed on schedule. _Whistle Down the Wind_ was a financial and critical success for producers Allied Film Makers, as was Basil Dearden and Michael Relph’s 1961 release _Victim_, and the two films found themselves in competition with each other for selection as the British entry to international film festivals.

The London trade show for _Whistle Down the Wind_ was on 19 July 1961 and it received effusive reviews in the British press. From _The Guardian_’s headline ‘Just Short of Genius’ to Peter Burnup’s _News of the World_ write-up – ‘I found myself held in enchantment from beginning to end. See it as soon as you can’ – the critics had no hesitation in recommending the film both for its artistic and entertainment merits. Alexander Walker was a particular fan, describing it as ‘a shining, humorous, touching, truthful British film.’

There was plenty of praise for Forbes’s handling of both the narrative and the non-professional cast, _Tribune_ opining that he ‘certainly possesses gifts, conspicuous in the British cinema by their absence’, while Paul Dehn in the _Daily Herald_ called it a ‘modest British masterpiece … brilliantly direct[ed]’. There was praise for Forbes’s ‘sensitive and showmanlike direction’, and Patrick Gibbs in _The Daily Telegraph_ noted his ‘eye for the countryside and … magic touch with the children’.

American critics were equally positive when it opened across the Atlantic in April 1962. ‘One of the most enjoyable and heartwarming films we’ve ever seen’, wrote Bosley Crowther in _The New York Times_. There was a strong sense that its ‘Englishness’ was a major element of its appeal, Richard L. Coe noting that ‘Forbes has developed each scene with beautiful reticence … there are scenes of exquisitely soft, country England beauty’, ‘reticence’ clearly being deemed a national trait. In contrast, _The Hollywood Reporter_ highlighted the ‘tart and humorous screenplay … [that] handles a difficult subject with discipline and insight’.

That the critics found many different aspects of the film praiseworthy is testament to the range of talent and creativity brought together for its production. The acting, direction, writing, cinematography and music were all singled out by reviewers, as were its effective creation of ‘that secret world of childhood’ and its allegorical qualities. The fact that the former was achieved without a trace of mawkishness and the latter without offending religious audiences was attributed to Forbes’s skill as a director.
